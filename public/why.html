Why it matters

The web just started charging AI to read it.

For thirty years the web was free to crawl. That is ending — not gradually, but on a date. The prices and refusals are being set machine-to-machine, in headers no one reads. This is the record of them.

The shift

Reading the web is becoming a transaction, not a right.

From 15 September 2026, Cloudflare blocks AI crawlers by default across much of the web and offers publishers a pay-per-crawl toll in the same dashboard. TollBit already runs a bot paywall on thousands of publisher sites. Major outlets have signed private licensing deals with AI companies; others return a payment demand to any crawler that knocks.

None of this is speculative, and none of it is visible in the way markets are normally visible. There is no ticker. A price quoted to an AI crawler appears for an instant in an HTTP response and is gone. A publisher's policy can change overnight and nobody outside that publisher would know.

The gap

Every new market needs a neutral price of record before it can function. This one doesn't have one yet.

Oil had no reference price until Platts started publishing assessments; fertiliser had none until Argus did. The reference publisher takes money from no participant, which is exactly what lets everyone cite it. The crawl economy is at that pre-reference moment now — real transactions, no independent record. Standing at the toll booth and writing down what passes is unglamorous and, so far, undone at scale.

What becomes answerable

Questions that had no data behind them, now do.

  • Is my sector walling up faster than the rest of the web?Block rates by crawler and by country, re-read weekly across the top 50,000 domains.
  • Which publishers have actually started charging — and how much?Every observed machine-readable price, and every payment wall that quotes no price, recorded with the date it appeared.
  • Did this domain change its AI policy the week that deal was announced?A dated history that cannot be back-filled: the one thing a competitor who starts later can never reconstruct.
  • Is a licensing deal visible on the wire, or only in the press release?Identity-conditional access — sites that serve one AI crawler and refuse another — measured directly.
  • How much of the web is one toggle away from charging?The share of domains already behind a CDN that can switch on pay-per-crawl in a single setting.

A reporter finds the story; an analyst finds the trend line; a publisher finds out where they stand against their peers; an AI team finds out what access will cost. The data is the same. The questions are yours.

The legal backdrop

The signal we measure is now the one the law looks at.

Whether a site allows AI to read it has moved from a technical courtesy to a compliance question, and recent rulings turned the machine-readable signal — the very thing this index records — into the legally operative one.

  • EU: a machine-readable opt-out is the one that counts.Under Article 4 of the EU Copyright Directive (2019/790), commercial text-and-data mining is permitted unless rights are reserved “in an appropriate manner, such as machine-readable means.” In Kneschke v. LAION (10 December 2025), the Hamburg Higher Regional Court held that a natural-language reservation in terms of service is insufficient: the opt-out must be machine-readable — robots.txt, an X-Robots-Tag header, or the TDM Reservation Protocol. Those are precisely the mechanisms this index reads every week.
  • EU AI Act: training-data transparency is in force.Since 2 August 2025, providers of general-purpose AI models must document training data and comply with EU copyright law, including honouring these reservations (AI Act, GPAI obligations). The 2026 Digital Omnibus adjusted some high-risk deadlines; the GPAI transparency obligations were not among them.
  • A pending EU referral may set the standard web-wide.The Court of Justice of the EU is considering case C-250/25 (Like Company v. Google), which may give authoritative guidance on what counts as a valid machine-readable reservation for AI training.
  • US: the market-defining case is still open.In The New York Times v. OpenAI & Microsoft (S.D.N.Y., 1:23-cv-11195), the core copyright claims survived dismissal and the case remains in active proceedings — the most-watched test of whether training on copyrighted text is fair use. US courts have separately described robots.txt as lacking direct legal force, which is part of why a neutral record of who sets which policy, and when, matters.

We state these as dated facts and link the primary sources. We do not predict how any case or rule will resolve — an observatory records the signal; it does not forecast the verdict.

What this is

An independent observatory, not a participant.

The Crawl Price Index reads the machine-readable policy of the top 50,000 domains every week and records what it finds: who blocks which AI crawlers, who charges, what the price is, and what changed since last week. It takes no money from anyone it measures and brokers no deals — the same discipline that lets a price index be cited by the market it observes.

Headline figures are free to cite with attribution. The full per-domain record, the country editions, and the accumulating history are the subscription. The method is documented in full, the crawler is cryptographically identifiable, and every edition is dated and reproducible.

The market is being built now. The record starts now too.
